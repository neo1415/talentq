1. What SongScribe is

SongScribe is a website where a person describes a song they want, and an AI writes and records it for them in about a minute. The person might want a birthday song for their mum with her name in it, an anthem for their small business, a wedding proposal track, or a jingle for a podcast. They choose the occasion, who it is for, the mood, the vocal style and the genre, and either paste their own lyrics or let the AI write them. A minute later they get two versions to listen to, and if they pay, they can download the MP3 and get a licence certificate saying what they are allowed to do with it.

There are two ways to build the brief. The wizard is an eight-step form. The AI Producer is a chat, by text or by voice, where an AI "producer" asks questions and proposes the brief, and nothing is spent until the person taps a confirm card that shows the price.

The product is aimed at Nigeria and the UK first (it prices in naira and pounds as well as dollars, and it integrates Flutterwave alongside Stripe), but there is nothing region-locked about it. The company name on invoices is SongScribe AI Ltd; the GitLab group is kuuza-ai.

Behind the curtain, SongScribe does not generate music itself. It pays a third party (a Suno reseller, musicapi.org / sunoapi.org) per generation, pays Google Gemini for lyrics and cover art, pays ElevenLabs for the voice conversation, and pays Anthropic as a backup brain for the producer. SongScribe's value is the guided experience, the licensing, and the packaging, not the model.

2. How it makes money

There are two overlapping pricing systems in the code, because the product is halfway through a migration from one to the other. You need to understand both.

The old system: plans with counters

A customer buys a tier. The tiers table holds them: Personal, Standard, Business, Exclusive, Studio Pro (a monthly subscription), plus a free Founder tier for staff. Buying one creates an order with counters on it: how many songs, variations, revisions and style boosts the order allows, and how many have been used. Every generation increments a counter. When songs are used up, the person buys another plan or an add-on (extra variations or revisions).

A variation is "generate again with the same brief"; a revision is "change the brief and generate again". Each generate call returns two tracks and counts as two variations.

The new system: credits

From August 2026 the code has a full credits ledger. Every action has a price in credits in the credit_costs table (a generation is 1,000 credits; lyrics drafting, cover art, stems and style boost each have their own prices). Credits arrive in lots that can expire, and are spent oldest-expiring first. New accounts get 3,000 free credits on signup. Customers top up by buying credit packs priced in USD, NGN, GBP or EUR.

A single environment variable, CREDITS_ENFORCEMENT, decides how much of this is real:

  • off: credits are ignored entirely.
  • shadow (the default): every action is priced and recorded in the ledger, but nobody is refused for lack of credits and the old counters still decide. This lets the team see what the new system would have done.
  • enforce: credits decide. A 402 response with a top-up offer appears when the balance is too low.

The code repeatedly refers to "the flip", meaning the day this switch goes to enforce. As of the last commit, that has not happened as far as the code can tell.

The funnel

Anonymous visitors can try a demo without an account: up to ten variations a day per IP, previews capped at sixty seconds or half the track, no downloads. Demo songs live in the visitor's browser storage for 48 hours. Signing up claims them into the account; paying (with a plan slot or with credits) "unlocks" them for download. This try-before-you-buy path is the main acquisition tool and shows up everywhere in the code as isDemo / mode=demo.

3. The shape of the system

SongScribe is one Next.js application deployed on Vercel. There is no separate backend service. The browser talks to Next.js pages and API routes; those talk to Supabase (database, login, file storage), Upstash Redis (short-lived counters and locks), and the external AI and payment providers. Providers call back into the app through webhooks.

Inside the app the code is layered like this, and once you see it the directory tree makes sense:

src/app/(group)/…/page.tsxPages. Server components that check who is signed in, fetch what the page needs, and hand it to a client component. src/app/api/**/route.tsServer endpoints. Every action that costs money, touches another user, or talks to a provider goes through one of these. src/lib/**The engine room. Pure logic and provider clients. Pages and routes are meant to be thin and call into here. src/components/**React UI. Feature folders (create, producer, library, credits…) plus a ui/ folder of shadcn primitives. src/store/**Five Zustand stores: browser-side state that several components share. src/hooks/**Client hooks that own network conversations (the producer session, voice, notifications). supabase/migrationsThe database schema as a numbered history of SQL files. A lot of business logic lives here as SQL functions.

Three principles run through the whole codebase and are worth naming now, because they explain many decisions you will meet:

  1. The server is authoritative. Prices, quotas, the producer's brief, and whether a song is downloadable are decided on the server. The browser only displays and asks.
  2. Everything that spends is idempotent. Webhooks retry, users double-click, and polls race the webhook, so every completion path has a dedupe key or a lock so that running it twice does nothing the second time.
  3. Defence in depth. Row Level Security in Postgres, a CSRF origin check in the proxy, rate limits in Redis, and per-route auth checks all overlap on purpose.

4. Words you will hear

Plain-English definitions of the technical terms this codebase uses constantly. Skim now, come back when you meet one.

Next.js App Router, server components, client components
Next.js is the React framework the app is built on. In the App Router, a file called page.tsx inside src/app/some/path/ becomes the page at /some/path. By default these components run on the server: they can read the database directly and send finished HTML. A file that starts with 'use client' runs in the browser instead and can use state, clicks and effects. The pattern here is: server page fetches data, client component handles interaction.
Route groups
Folders in parentheses like (marketing) or (shell) do not appear in the URL. They exist so different sections can share different layouts: marketing pages get a header and footer, shell pages get the sidebar.
Route handler / API route
A route.ts file exporting GET, POST, etc. It is a server endpoint the browser calls with fetch. All money-spending actions go through these.
Proxy (middleware)
Code that runs before every request. Next.js 16 calls it proxy.ts (it used to be middleware.ts). Here it refreshes the login session, redirects signed-out visitors away from protected pages, and blocks cross-site POSTs.
Supabase
A hosted Postgres database with login (Auth), file storage, and a REST API. The app uses all three. There are two clients: the normal one, which acts as the signed-in user and is limited by RLS, and the admin or service-role client, which bypasses RLS and is only used on the server for things like webhooks.
RLS (Row Level Security)
Rules inside Postgres that say which rows a given user may see or change. For example, "a user can select from songs only where user_id equals their own id". Even a bug in the app cannot leak another person's songs, because the database itself refuses.
RPC, SQL function, SECURITY DEFINER
An RPC is a function that lives inside the database and is called from the app with supabase.rpc('name', args). SECURITY DEFINER means the function runs with the privileges of whoever created it (the admin), not the caller. This is how the app does things a normal user is not allowed to do directly, such as spend credits, in one atomic step. Because such functions are powerful, several migrations exist only to revoke the right to call them from the public roles.
Migration
A numbered SQL file that changes the database schema. They are applied in order, once each, and never edited after they ship. The folder supabase/migrations is the true history of the data model.
Redis (Upstash)
A fast key-value store used for things that must be quick and short-lived: "how many requests has this IP made this minute", "which producer sessions are live", "is this generation already being finalised". Data here can be lost without breaking anything important; Postgres is the record.
Rate limiter
A counter that refuses the Nth request in a time window and returns a 429 status. Here they protect every expensive route.
Webhook
A URL the app exposes so an outside service can call us when something happens: Stripe says "payment succeeded", the music provider says "your track is done". Webhooks must verify a signature so nobody can fake them, and must tolerate being called twice.
Idempotent / dedupe key
An operation is idempotent if doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. A dedupe key is a unique string stored with the first attempt so a second attempt recognises itself and stops. You will see keys like song.generate:<generationId> everywhere in the credits code.
TOCTOU race
"Time of check to time of use". You check "has quota", then you use it, but between those two steps another request also checked and used. The fix is doing check and use in one atomic database statement. The codebase has fixed most of these and admits to one it has not.
Signed URL
A temporary link to a private file in storage, valid for a few minutes. Audio and images are private in Supabase Storage; the app mints signed URLs on demand so only entitled people can fetch them.
CSRF, Origin header
Cross-site request forgery is another website making your browser POST to SongScribe with your cookies. The proxy rejects any mutating API call whose Origin header is not SongScribe's own domain.
Nonce
A random one-time token. The producer's confirm card carries one; redeeming it is what authorises a spend, and it can only be redeemed once.
Zustand
A tiny React state library. A "store" is an object with values and setter functions that any component can subscribe to. Some stores persist to sessionStorage or localStorage.
TanStack Query
A library for fetching and caching server data in the browser. Installed and provided in providers.tsx but used sparingly.
zod
A schema validation library. Request bodies from the browser and responses from providers are checked against zod schemas so bad shapes fail loudly at the boundary.
NDJSON, SSE, streaming
Ways to send a response piece by piece. The producer chat streams the model's words as they are generated: NDJSON is one JSON object per line; SSE ("server-sent events") is the format ElevenLabs and OpenAI-compatible clients expect.
Tool loop / function calling
The producer gives the language model a list of "tools" it may call (update the brief, propose lyrics…). The model replies with a tool call instead of prose; the server executes it, feeds the result back, and lets the model continue. That repeat is the loop.
shadcn/ui, Tailwind
Tailwind is utility-class CSS (flex items-center px-4). shadcn/ui is a set of copy-pasted React components (Button, Dialog, Slider…) that live in src/components/ui and are yours to edit.
Vitest, MSW, Playwright
Vitest runs unit tests. MSW (Mock Service Worker) intercepts network calls in tests so nothing real is hit; it is configured to fail any test that makes an unexpected request. Playwright drives a real browser for end-to-end tests, run only in CI.

5. The main journeys, step by step

These are the paths a real person takes through the product, traced through the code. Each step names the file that does the work so you can open it alongside.

5.1 A visitor tries the demo without an account

  1. They land on the home page (src/app/(marketing)/page.tsx) and click "Try the free demo", which links to /create?mode=demo.
  2. The proxy lets them through. src/proxy.ts asks src/lib/route-access.ts whether the path needs login. /create and /create/manual are on the demo allowlist when ?mode=demo is present; /create/ai never is. The proxy also sets an ss_country cookie from Vercel's country header for pricing later.
  3. The chooser page (src/app/(create)/create/page.tsx) offers "Manual wizard" or "AI Producer". For anonymous users, the Producer card routes to login.
  4. The wizard runs (src/components/create/song-wizard.tsx) with isDemo true. Every answer is saved to the song-form-store in sessionStorage so a reload does not lose the form.
  5. Lyrics are drafted by POST /api/lyrics/generate. For a demo it is metered per IP by the lyrics rate limiter (5 per minute) and produces up to 2 variations via Gemini.
  6. Generation is requested by POST /api/generate with isDemo: true. The route checks the demo_usage table for that IP (10 variations per day), creates a songs row with user_id null and is_demo true, a generations row, and dispatches to the music provider through src/lib/music/dispatch.ts.
  7. The browser polls GET /api/generate?taskId=… every few seconds (step-generate.tsx). When the provider is done, the same idempotent completeGeneration that the webhook uses copies the audio into private storage and marks the generation ready.
  8. The song is remembered locally. The IDs go into the demo-songs-store in localStorage with a 48-hour TTL. Playback goes through /api/audio/stream, which serves a demo track in full to the browser but the players cap playback at 60 seconds or half the length with a fade (DEMO_LIMITS in constants.ts).
  9. They are nudged to sign up by the sidebar card ("Create a free account to keep the songs you make") and by demo-song-claimer.tsx, which after signup posts the stored IDs to /api/songs/claim-demo so the songs move into the new account.

5.2 Sign up and sign in

  1. Pages live in src/app/(auth)/: login, signup, forgot-password, update-password. They call Supabase Auth directly from the browser using src/lib/supabase/client.ts. Email+password and Google OAuth are both offered.
  2. OAuth and email links come back through src/app/auth/callback/route.ts, which exchanges the code for a session and redirects to the redirect query param (validated to be a relative path).
  3. A profile row is created by a database trigger on auth.users insert (in the initial migration), and since August a second trigger grants the 3,000-credit signup lot with a dedupe key grant.signup:<userId>.
  4. The session is refreshed on every request by proxy.ts, which is what keeps cookies valid. The root layout (src/app/layout.tsx) reads the user on the server and hydrates the auth-store so client components know who is signed in on first paint.
  5. Signing out is POST /api/auth/signout plus src/lib/sign-out.ts, which clears every SongScribe key in browser storage (clear-user-data.ts) so the next user on a shared machine does not inherit a half-filled form.
  6. Password change happens through POST /api/auth/change-password, rate limited by the auth limiter (3 per window), and the update-password page uses a server action.
  7. Account deletion is POST /api/account/delete: it deletes the user's storage objects, then the auth user (cascading rows), using the admin client.

5.3 Buying a plan, an add-on, or a credit pack

  1. Prices are never in the browser. The pricing page ((marketing)/pricing/page.tsx) calls src/lib/currency/product-pricing.ts on the server, which reads product_prices and formats strings for the visitor's currency. Currency comes from resolveCurrency in src/lib/currency/index.ts: profile preference, then the ss_country cookie, then Vercel's country header, then USD.
  2. The checkout page ((app)/checkout/page.tsx + checkout-client.tsx) shows the tier, add-ons, and a payment-method picker (components/checkout/payment-method-picker.tsx). Naira is Flutterwave only; other currencies can use Stripe or Flutterwave.
  3. Plans: POST /api/checkout creates a pending order row (one per user, enforced by a unique index) and then either a Stripe Checkout Session (src/lib/stripe.ts) or a Flutterwave payment link (src/lib/flutterwave.ts) with tx_ref prefixed SS-. Checkout is rate limited to 3 per window.
  4. Packs: POST /api/billing/checkout does the same for credit packs (src/lib/credits/packs.ts), writing a credit_purchases row and using tx_ref prefix SSPACK-. Stripe pack sessions ask Stripe to create an invoice too.
  5. The customer pays on the provider's page and is redirected back: Stripe to /api/checkout/success or /api/billing/checkout/return, Flutterwave to the same return routes with its transaction id.
  6. Settlement happens twice on purpose: once on the redirect (so the customer sees success immediately) and once on the webhook (/api/webhooks/stripe, /api/webhooks/flutterwave) in case the redirect never happened. Both call the same code: order activation for plans, and settlePurchase in src/lib/credits/settle-purchase.ts for packs, which grants the credit lot with a dedupe key so the second call is a no-op. Flutterwave payments are always re-verified server-side before anything is granted.
  7. Follow-ups: add-on bonuses are applied by the apply_addon_bonuses SQL function, an upgrade from one active plan to another carries remaining quota via handle_order_upgrade, orphaned demo songs are claimed via claim_orphaned_songs, and a receipt email is sent through src/lib/email/dispatch.ts.
  8. Refunds: Stripe's charge.refunded event reclaims the unspent part of a pack's lot via the reclaim_credit_lot RPC. There is no equivalent for Flutterwave refunds.
  9. Afterwards the person can see the statement, purchases, and download their own PDF invoice from /billing (components/credits/billing-client.tsx, /api/billing/statement, /api/billing/invoice/[id]).

5.4 Making a song with the wizard

  1. Eight steps, each a component in src/components/create/steps/: intent, recipient, mood, vocals, style, lyrics, review-lyrics, generate. song-wizard.tsx owns the step counter, the Next/Back rules from src/lib/create-validation.ts, and skips the review step when lyrics are custom or the song is instrumental.
  2. Answers are typed by SongFormData in src/types/index.ts, validated by the zod schema in src/lib/song-form-schema.ts, and drawn from the option lists in src/lib/constants.ts (intents, mood groups, style groups, vocal types, special elements, length preferences).
  3. Arriving with context: ?orderId= ties the song to a plan, ?songId= makes it a revision of an existing song (pre-filled from the database), ?sampleId= pre-fills from one of the 24 showcase samples in src/lib/sample-songs.ts.
  4. Lyrics step calls POST /api/lyrics/generate, which builds a Gemini prompt (buildGeminiLyricsPrompt in prompt-builder.ts) asking for structured JSON with several titled variations. The person picks one and can edit it. A snapshot of the form is saved so changing an earlier answer regenerates automatically.
  5. Generate step shows a staged animation and posts to POST /api/generate. That route is the busiest file in the app. It: authenticates; rate limits; checks entitlement against the order counters (src/lib/entitlements.ts) or demo usage; runs the credits preflight; builds the provider payload (buildSunoPrompt, generate-payload.ts); inserts songs and generations rows; dispatches; charges credits after acceptance; and returns a task id.
  6. Dispatch goes through src/lib/music/: the router picks a provider (only musicapi exists today), the budget checks in-flight capacity in Redis, the limiter throttles submissions (18 per 10 seconds globally), and failures mark health strikes so a sick provider is skipped.
  7. Completion arrives either by webhook (/api/webhooks/suno or /api/webhooks/music/[provider], both delegating to src/lib/music/callback-handler.ts) or by the browser's poll of GET /api/generate. Both call completeGeneration in src/lib/suno/complete-generation.ts, which takes a Redis lock, downloads each track into the private audio-files bucket (src/lib/suno/storage.ts), stores lyrics and cover art URLs in the generation's metadata, flips statuses to ready, increments the order counters, writes a notification and sends the "song ready" email.
  8. Failures mark the generation failed, refund credits if any were charged, and write a "song failed" notification. Artist-name rejections from the provider are not retried.
  9. The person is sent to /library/songs/[id] to listen, or, for a demo, kept in the wizard with the demo player.

5.5 Making a song with the AI Producer

This is the most sophisticated part of the codebase, and it is built around one rule: the model may only propose; the person confirms; the server spends.

  1. Open /create/ai (signed in only). Its layout sets a Permissions-Policy that allows the microphone on this route alone. The sidebar deliberately links to the chooser with a full page load so the header is fresh.
  2. Bootstrap: use-producer-session.ts posts to /api/producer/session. The server creates a session in Redis (session-store.ts) with a fresh spec, admits it under the budgets in session-budget.ts (25 live sessions globally, 1 per user, 60 turns, 250k tokens, 60 minutes text or 15 minutes voice), and returns a projection (projection.ts) of the state for the UI. Returning users see a picker of past conversations (conversation-picker.tsx, backed by /api/producer/conversations and the producer_sessions archive table). First-timers see a short onboarding survey (onboarding-survey.tsx, saved by /api/producer/attribution).
  3. Each text turn posts to /api/producer/chat, which streams NDJSON back. Inside, engine.ts runs the tool loop: it builds the system prompt (system-prompt.ts, with vocabulary from vocabulary.ts and a phase from state-machine.ts), calls the LLM through llm/index.ts (Gemini Flash by default, Anthropic on failure), and executes any tool calls through tool-runtime.ts. Tools (tools.ts) are: update the spec, propose lyrics, select lyrics, propose generation, cancel proposal, hand off to the wizard. At most three tool iterations per turn.
  4. The spec (spec.ts) is the brief: the same fields the wizard collects. Every change is merged three-way (merge-turn.ts) so text and voice turns arriving at once do not clobber each other, and the session revision number increases so the browser can ignore stale state.
  5. A proposal becomes a confirm card in the chat with a nonce and a price locked from the price list (pricing.ts, price-lock.ts). The card expires after 120 seconds and is bound to a hash of the spec, so editing the brief after the card appears invalidates it.
  6. Confirm: the browser posts the nonce to /api/producer/confirm (proposal.ts redeems it exactly once), then itself calls the ordinary /api/lyrics/generate or /api/generate with the payload the server returned, then reports what happened to /api/producer/session-event. Generation progress is polled by the same hook, and on reload the watch is resumed.
  7. Voice: voice-surface.tsx and use-producer-voice.ts ask /api/producer/voice for a signed ElevenLabs URL plus an HMAC session token (session-token.ts). ElevenLabs then talks to /api/webhooks/producer/[[...path]], which pretends to be an OpenAI chat-completions endpoint (openai-adapter.ts), verifies a bearer secret and the session token, and runs the same engine. Because voice turns happen entirely server-side, the browser polls /api/producer/state?since= to keep the spec panel current.
  8. Endings: a conversation ends when a budget is hit; it stays readable, and "continue" starts a new session seeded with the old brief (resume.ts). Every turn is archived to producer_sessions (session-archive.ts). The daily cron (/api/cron/producer-retention) deletes abandoned conversations after 30 days and song-producing ones after a year.

5.6 How a credit spend actually happens

  1. Price lookup. src/lib/credits/actions.ts maps an action name like song.generate to its row in credit_costs.
  2. Preflight. Before calling a provider, the route calls preflightCredits in enforcement.ts. In enforce mode it reads the balance (balance.ts) and returns a structured 402 (out-of-credits.ts) if short. In shadow it only logs.
  3. Charge after acceptance. Once the provider accepts the job, chargeAfterDispatch calls the spend_credits RPC (spend.ts). That SQL function takes an advisory lock per user, checks the balance, picks lots by soonest expiry, writes credit_allocations against them, and appends one credit_ledger row with the dedupe key. Calling it again with the same key returns the same result without spending.
  4. Refund on failure. If the generation fails, refund_credits is called with a key derived from the spend, and the ledger gets a matching positive row.
  5. Statement. statement.ts turns the ledger into the human-readable list on /billing, pairing refunds with their spends and naming the song involved.
  6. Demo unlock. A demo song can be kept for credits: unlock.ts prices it and /api/songs/unlock calls the unlock_demo_song RPC, which spends and flips is_demo in one transaction.

5.7 Stems, cover art, style boost

After a song is ready, the song page shows a Tools panel (components/library/song-tools.tsx) whose availability comes from the tier's feature flags (getUserFeatures in entitlements.ts).

  • Stems (tool-stems.tsx, /api/studio/stems): asks the provider to separate vocals from instrumental. An operations row tracks it; /api/studio/operations/[id] is polled; complete-operation.ts stores the resulting files; /api/audio/stems streams them.
  • Cover art (tool-cover-art.tsx, /api/studio/cover): generates images through Gemini's image model with a prompt from buildCoverArtPrompt. /api/songs/[id]/cover sets the chosen one; /api/images proxies private images.
  • Style boost (tool-style-boost.tsx, /api/studio/style-boost and …/regenerate): asks the provider to enrich the genre description, then regenerates the song with it. Metered by the order's style-boost counter and priced in credits.

Note: these four routes still call the old sunoClient directly instead of the newer provider abstraction. That matters for the assessment later.

5.8 Library, sharing, licence, invoices

  • Library ((shell)/library/page.tsx + library-client.tsx): all of the person's songs with search, filters, favourites and stat cards. Data shaping is in src/lib/library.ts. Deletions are soft (a deleted_at stamp).
  • Song page (library/songs/[id]/page.tsx): the player with every generation attempt, lyrics, the prompt that was sent, metadata, add-ons, the tools panel, revision and delete actions, and the demo banners that offer to claim or unlock.
  • Playback: a persistent bottom player (ui/persistent-player.tsx, player-store.ts) takes over from inline players when it is mounted. Plays are counted through src/lib/plays.ts with a Redis cooldown so refreshing does not inflate counts.
  • Sharing: share-song-toggle.tsx creates a token; /s/[token] is a public page with a call to action for visitors.
  • Licence PDF: /api/songs/[id]/license renders src/lib/license-pdf.tsx using the wording in policies/licenses.txt and the tier's licence type.
  • Invoice PDF: /api/billing/invoice/[id] renders src/lib/invoice-pdf.tsx on demand for any payment.
  • Download all: download-all-button.tsx zips variations in the browser with JSZip.

5.9 Notifications and email

  • Notifications are rows in notifications; a null user_id means a broadcast to everyone. Read state lives in notification_reads. Kinds today: song ready, song failed, order activated, announcement, and a trending kind nothing writes yet.
  • The sidebar badge (use-unread-notifications.ts) is seeded on the server and kept live over Supabase Realtime for personal rows, with a recount on every navigation to catch broadcasts.
  • The page (notifications-client.tsx) pages with a cursor and marks read optimistically via /api/notifications/read.
  • Broadcasts are sent with scripts/announce.mjs, never from the UI, because the table deliberately has no user insert policy.
  • Email goes through Resend (src/lib/email/resend.ts) for order receipts, song ready, welcome-once (stamped in welcome_email_sent_at), and pack receipts, each with an idempotency key. The contact form uses ZeptoMail instead (zeptomail.ts, /api/contact).

9. Running it and working day to day

  1. Install. Node 20+, then npm install in the clone.
  2. Environment. Copy .env.local.example to .env.local. You will need the real keys from whoever owns the Supabase, Upstash and provider accounts. Nothing in the repo contains a secret.
  3. Database. Either point at the shared remote project, or run supabase start (needs Docker) and npm run db:local, which writes an override file so your dev server uses the local stack. Migrations apply automatically to the local stack.
  4. Run. npm run dev. Webhooks cannot reach your laptop, which is fine: the browser poll finalises generations on its own.
  5. Before every push. npm run verify runs exactly what CI runs: typecheck, lint, Prettier check, and the unit tests. Prettier failures are the most common CI red.
  6. Branching. Work on daniel/dev (or feature branches named songscribe/<thing> to match the existing convention), open merge requests into dev, and let main take merges from dev when deploying.
  7. Schema changes. Add a new timestamped file in supabase/migrations, apply it locally, run npm run db:types, commit both. Never edit an old migration.
  8. Announcements and one-off grants use the scripts in section 7.8 and need the service-role key.

11. My assessment

I will start with what is good, because a lot is, then go through the problems in order of how much they matter.

11.1 What is genuinely good

  • Money is handled carefully. Idempotent settlement shared by webhook and redirect, dedupe keys on every ledger row, advisory locks in spend_credits, verify-then-settle for Flutterwave, refunds that return credits to the right lot, prices in the database rather than in components. This is better than most early-stage products.
  • The AI Producer is well designed. The model can only propose. Confirmation is a one-time nonce bound to a spec hash with a locked price. Sessions have budgets. Text and voice share one engine. The client store never edits server truth. These are the right decisions, and they are tested (24 test files).
  • Security posture is above average. RLS everywhere, definer functions closed to public roles, CSRF origin checks, rate limits per route, private storage with signed URLs, secrets compared in constant time, cron that fails closed, security headers, mic permission scoped to one route. The two e2e specs written after real incidents show the team learns from mistakes.
  • The migration from counters to credits is being done responsibly. Shadow mode, a keyed backfill with dry runs and production guards, a documented plan for "the flip".
  • The test harness is thoughtful. MSW fails on any unmocked request; the Anthropic and Gemini fakes emit real SSE so the streaming parsers are tested; the Redis mock honours TTL because admission control depends on it.
  • Provider drift is caught at the boundary. Every provider response is zod-validated.
  • Small UX decisions are considered. The sidebar cookie to avoid a first-paint flash, stale-response guards on search, optimistic reads, "9+" badges, players that cap demo playback with a fade.

11.2 Bugs and risks I found

Ordered by how much I would worry. Severity is my judgement.

Critical

The GitHub mirror is public

Not a code bug, but the most urgent item. github.com/neo1415/songscribe exposes the full source, the internal claude.md, pricing logic, provider names, and the shape of every security control. No secrets are in the repo, but this is still a gift to a competitor and a map for an attacker. Make it private.

High

Flutterwave webhook can create an order for the wrong account

When a Flutterwave payment arrives whose tx_ref does not match a pending order, the webhook falls back to finding the user by the customer email Flutterwave reports, and can create a new order for them. Email is not a reliable identity: a customer paying with a different email than their account, or two accounts sharing a payment email, would get credit in the wrong place. The primary path (bind by verified transaction to the pending order) is fine; the fallback should log and stop, or land in a manual review queue, not act.

src/app/api/webhooks/flutterwave/route.ts (user lookup by email, order creation branch)

High

Flutterwave order amounts are not currency-aware

The webhook stores amount_cents: Math.round(amount * 100). For NGN that is fine in practice, but the same code would run for any currency Flutterwave sends, including zero-decimal currencies, and it disagrees with the checkout route which already computes cents correctly from product_prices. Amount comparisons between what was expected and what was paid should be done in minor units from one function.

src/app/api/webhooks/flutterwave/route.ts:279, :304

Medium

"Try again" on the checkout status page links to a broken URL

The status page builds /checkout?tier=${orderId}. The checkout page expects a tier id like standard, not an order UUID, so the retry lands on an invalid tier. A customer whose payment failed is sent somewhere that does not work at exactly the moment they were willing to pay.

src/app/(app)/checkout/status/page.tsx:64, :87

Medium

Demo quota has an admitted race

The route comments say so: the demo usage check is a select followed by an update, so parallel requests from one IP can exceed the daily ten. The cost is provider spend on free users. The atomic upsert migration exists for the increment, but the decision to allow is still made on the earlier read. A single "check and increment or refuse" SQL function closes it.

src/app/api/generate/route.ts:112

Medium

Four studio routes bypass the provider seam

studio/stems, studio/style-boost, studio/style-boost/regenerate, studio/operations/[id] and songs/claim-demo import sunoClient directly. They get none of the failover, health strikes, or capacity budgeting the generate path has, and adding a second provider will not help them. Either extend the provider interface to cover stems, cover and style boost, or explicitly document that those features are Suno-only.

grep -rl sunoClient src/app/api

Medium

Style boost moves the legacy counter before credits can refuse

This is documented as deliberate: the check-and-increment RPC runs, then the credits preflight, so in enforce mode a refused boost still consumes a legacy style boost. The comment argues the counter is about to be dropped. That is a reasonable trade if the flip happens soon; if it slips, customers on plans lose boosts they never received. Worth a date on it.

src/app/api/studio/style-boost/route.ts:60-80

Medium

Redis failures mostly fail open

Rate limiters and several guards catch Redis errors and allow the request so an Upstash outage does not take the site down. That is a defensible availability choice, but it means the day Redis is down is also the day nothing is rate limited, producer admission is unbounded, and generation locks do not hold. At minimum this should alert loudly; for the spend routes it should probably fail closed.

src/lib/rate-limit.ts, src/lib/music/budget.ts, src/lib/producer/session-budget.ts

Medium

No Flutterwave refund handling

Stripe's charge.refunded reclaims unspent credits. A Flutterwave refund leaves the credits in the account. With NGN being Flutterwave-only, this is the market where it matters most.

Low

Invoice issuer details are incomplete

The invoice PDF names SongScribe AI Ltd with an empty address and no VAT or registration number. For UK customers, and for any business buyer expensing the purchase, that is not a valid invoice. Fill it from configuration, not code.

src/lib/invoice-pdf.tsx

Low

Analytics identifiers are hardcoded

The GTM container id and the Meta pixel id are literals in the root layout, so every local dev session and preview deploy reports into production analytics. Move them to NEXT_PUBLIC_* variables and leave them unset outside production.

src/app/layout.tsx

Low

Documentation drift

README.md is boilerplate. claude.md mentions a dashboard page that is now a redirect, "20 migrations" when there are 57, and Polar which was replaced in February. The FAQ says previews are "streaming only" and that variations per day is ten, which is true, but also says WAV downloads exist on higher tiers, which nothing in the code delivers.

Low

Unused tables and dead surface

payment_methods and assets have no meaningful writers. The song_trending notification kind, the Explore nav item, and the "AI Producer route allows the microphone" e2e test are all placeholders for work not started. Harmless individually; together they make the schema look bigger than it is.

Low

One light-theme leftover

The checkout status page uses default shadcn colour classes (bg-background, text-muted-foreground) while every other screen is styled with literal dark hex values. It looks like a different app for the ten seconds a customer is most anxious.

11.3 Code quality

Overall: good, with a distinctive and somewhat costly style. TypeScript is strict, there are zero TODO markers, boundaries are validated, and the test count is real. The concerns are about weight and consistency rather than correctness.

  • The comments are enormous. The generate route is 753 lines, of which 213 are comments. Files routinely carry paragraphs narrating the bug that led to a line, what the previous version did, and why an alternative was rejected. This is more useful than no comments, and some are excellent (the producer hook, the client-value hook). But it triples the reading time of every file, it goes stale the moment the code moves, and it reads as commit-message history stored in the wrong place. My honest read is that much of the August and September code was written with heavy AI assistance, which produces exactly this voice. That is not a criticism of the result, but you should expect to trim rather than add when you touch these files, and you should put history in commit messages and ADRs, not inline.
  • Several files are too big to hold in your head. step-generate.tsx (1,335 lines) mixes the loading animation, polling, result rendering, demo player and claim flow. billing-client.tsx (1,098) is the whole billing page. generate/route.ts (753) does entitlement, credits, payload, dispatch and completion. app-shell.tsx (739). Each should be three to five files.
  • Two eras, two styles. February code uses any-adjacent Supabase results and hand-typed rows; August code introduces zod and explicit types but the generated database types are committed and not used, so every query is still loosely typed. The song page hand-declares ActiveOrderRow for a reason the comment explains: the generated types model a to-one join as an array. That is a known Supabase quirk with a known fix (type helpers), and adopting the generated types module by module would remove a whole class of "NaN songs left" bugs the comments describe having fixed by hand.
  • Styling is literal hex everywhere. #e63946, rgba(255,255,255,0.05), and pixel sizes copied from Figma appear hundreds of times in class strings. There is a Tailwind theme with tokens, and it is bypassed. A brand colour change is a repository-wide find and replace. Define brand, surface, line tokens and use them.
  • Client fetch code is repeated. A dozen components each hand-roll fetch + res.json() + error toast. TanStack Query is installed and provided and almost unused. Either use it for reads (library, notifications, conversations, billing) or remove it.
  • Tests are dense where money is and thin where users are. No route handler is unit tested (the comments argue Vitest cannot see cookies and Supabase, which is true only if you do not extract the decision logic). No page component is tested. The e2e suite is four specs and only runs in CI. A regression in the wizard or the library would not be caught by anything automated.
  • Good habits worth keeping: colocated tests, zod at boundaries, the ApiError class, dedupe keys named by convention (action:artefactId), the pure-function split (router, route-access, presentation) that makes logic testable without infrastructure.

11.4 Architecture

  • The fundamentals are right for the stage. One Next.js app, managed Postgres, managed Redis, managed auth, provider webhooks. No microservices, no queue, no Kubernetes. That is the correct amount of infrastructure for a two-person company, and it keeps the operating cost near zero until there is revenue to justify more.
  • The dual pricing system is the biggest architectural debt. Order counters and credit lots both run on every request. Every spend route has two guards, two error shapes, and comments explaining which one wins in which mode. The sidebar, the song page, the producer's pricing and the claim button all branch on it. Until the flip happens and the counters are deleted, every new feature costs double. I would set a date for the flip, do it, and spend the following month deleting: songs_used, variations_used, revisions_used, style_boosts_used, add-ons, check_style_boost_quota, the upgrade-carries-quota function, and the entitlement checks that read them.
  • Business logic is split between SQL and TypeScript without a rule. Spending, granting, claiming, unlocking, upgrading and add-on bonuses are SQL functions. Entitlement checks, demo limits and pricing resolution are TypeScript. Both are legitimate, but a newcomer cannot predict where to look. Write the rule down: anything that must be atomic across rows lives in SQL; everything else lives in src/lib.
  • Completion has two entry points by design (webhook and poll) sharing one idempotent finaliser. That is good. But the browser poll is load-bearing on localhost and in the producer, and a closed tab means nobody polls until the webhook arrives. If the webhook is misconfigured in production, songs silently never complete. A small server-side sweeper (cron every few minutes finalising any generation older than N minutes still marked generating) removes the dependency on either.
  • The music provider seam is half-built. The interface, registry, router and dispatch exist and are tested, but only one adapter exists and only the generate path uses the seam. Either finish it (add the second provider, route stems and covers through it) or accept that it is speculative and keep it small. Half-built abstractions are the most expensive kind.
  • Redis holds state that would be painful to lose. Live producer sessions and confirm nonces live only in Redis until archived. The archive happens every turn, so the loss window is small, but an Upstash incident mid-conversation ends every live session. Acceptable now; document it as a known limit.
  • There is no admin surface. Tier grants, announcements, refund investigation, looking up a customer's order: all SQL or scripts with the service-role key on a laptop. The announce script's own comments say why (an admin gate is security-critical and should be designed on purpose). Agreed, but at the point where a non-engineer needs to answer a support ticket, this becomes the bottleneck of the whole company.
  • Observability is console.log. Structured prefixes like [FLW Webhook] help, but there is no error tracker, no metrics, no alert when a webhook fails signature verification or a provider is struck out. Vercel logs are the only window. A Sentry-class tool and three alerts (webhook failures, provider health strikes, credits spend errors) would pay for themselves the first week.
  • Bus factor is one. Every commit is one person. There is no CODEOWNERS, no review history, no runbook. You are now the second person, and this handbook is the first document that is not the code itself.

11.5 Product and business

  • The margin structure is thin per song and depends on a reseller. The backfill script prices a credit at roughly 0.006 cents of provider cost, so a 1,000-credit generation costs about six cents to fulfil, against a Personal plan at $9.99 for one song. Gross margin on generation is excellent. The risk is upstream: the whole product routes through an unofficial Suno reseller. If that reseller loses access, or Suno changes terms, revenue stops the same afternoon. The provider seam exists precisely for this; finishing it and signing up a second provider is a business continuity task, not a code nicety.
  • Licensing claims outrun what the platform can guarantee. The Exclusive tier promises "your song will never be resold or reused by us" and "Content ID allowed". SongScribe does not control the underlying model, and AI-generated music's copyright status is unsettled in most jurisdictions. The licence text should be reviewed by a lawyer before the Exclusive tier is sold at scale, and the FAQ should not promise WAV files the code cannot produce.
  • The credits flip is a pricing change customers will feel. The backfill comments compute that early buyers paid three to four times more per credit than the new entry tier. A flat launch bonus is the chosen fix. Announce the flip clearly, in-app and by email, with the bonus visible in the statement, or the first month after the flip will be support tickets.
  • Two funnels, one metric missing. Demo → signup → pay is instrumented through GTM and the pixel, but nothing in the database records which path (wizard or producer) produced a paying customer, beyond the producer's first-touch attribution. Add a created_via on songs and orders so the question "does the producer convert better than the wizard" has an answer.
  • Nigeria-first is a real advantage and under-served in the UI. Naira pricing and Flutterwave are built, but the marketing copy, samples, and FAQ are written for a US audience ($199/mo, "YouTubers"). The samples list leans Afrobeat and Amapiano, which is right. Lean further: local occasions (naming ceremonies, owambe, send-forths), local payment reassurance, WhatsApp share.
  • Studio Pro "unlimited" is a liability under credits. The subscription promises unlimited songs against a per-song provider cost. The backfill maps it to 600,000 credits a month. Make that ceiling visible before someone automates against it.
  • Support is an email address. The FAQ says "Support Team: Online Now" over a stock photo. With no admin UI, every ticket is an engineer running SQL. Before any paid acquisition, budget for a support tool and the admin views to answer the top five ticket types: where is my song, why did my payment not activate, refund, change email, download licence again.
  • Explore is the growth feature the code keeps hinting at. Plays, favourites, sharing and a trending notification kind are all built. A public gallery of shared songs is the cheapest organic acquisition channel this product could have, and most of the plumbing is already there.
